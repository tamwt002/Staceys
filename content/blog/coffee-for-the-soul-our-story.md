Coffee is a funny thing. On paper it's roasted seeds and hot water. In real life it's the first warm thing you hold in the morning, the excuse to step away from your desk, the reason you finally talk to the person who's been sitting at the next table every Tuesday for a month.

We've spent a lot of time thinking about why a small cup of something brown matters so much to so many people. Turns out the answer is also the story of how this garden, and the old house in front of it, became Stacy's. So pull up a chair. This one's a bit long, and it starts with a broken heater.

## It started in a share house on a Tuesday

A few winters ago, four of us were living in a draughty terrace a few streets back from Brunswick Street. The heater had died. The landlord was "looking into it". So we did what anyone would do: we huddled around the kitchen bench and made coffee.

**Mia** had spent ten years making coffee in other people's cafes and owned an espresso machine older than all of us. **Theo** ran a record stall at a Sunday market and had more crates of vinyl than furniture. **Ana** had quietly propagated so many plant cuttings in jam jars that you couldn't see out the windows. And **Kofi** was a carpenter who couldn't walk past a skip bin without rescuing a chair.

That night the machine sputtered, hissed and then refused to work at all. Mia fixed it with a butter knife and a level of stubbornness we've since learned not to argue with. Theo put on a record. Ana made toasties. Kofi fixed the wobbly kitchen stool while we watched.

Somewhere around the third flat white, Ana said, "We should just do this for real."

Everyone laughed. Then nobody said anything for a while.

## The old house with the faded sign

A week later, walking home from the pub, we passed an old two storey house that had been empty for so long the *For Lease* sign out the front had faded to a pale pink. Peeling paint, a staircase that creaked on every step, and out the back, a garden so overgrown you could barely see the lemon tree in the middle of it.

Kofi called the number the next morning before anyone could talk him out of it.

The owner was a retired seamstress who'd raised three kids in that house and thrown, by her own account, some "very good parties" in the lounge room in the seventies. She walked us through every room, looked at the four of us standing in the garden in our op shop jumpers and said, "You look like trouble. Good. This house misses it." We shook on it over a cup of very bad instant coffee in her kitchen.

We pooled our savings, sold things we probably shouldn't have, and signed the lease with shaking hands.

## Building it by hand (and slightly crooked)

We didn't have money for a fit out, so we made one.

The garden came first. We spent a month pulling weeds, and Kofi built a little coffee bar out of old floorboards saved from a terrace being knocked down around the corner, right under the lemon tree. Every garden chair came from hard rubbish, a garage sale or a nan clearing out her shed. Ana's plants moved in and immediately started climbing the fences.

Then the house. Theo claimed the lounge room for the decks and lined the upstairs front room with crates of records and the saggiest couches we could find. The shelf up there is still noticeably wonky. We've decided it's a feature.

There were nights painting until 2am, a burst pipe we don't talk about, and one very tense afternoon trying to get a second hand espresso machine down the side path. Mia named the machine Doris. Doris is still going.

## Opening day: 31 coffees and one record

We opened the garden on a Saturday with no sign, no printed menu and a chalkboard propped against the back door that just said *coffee, toast, records*.

We sold 31 coffees. Theo played the same album nine times in a row through the kitchen window because none of us could work out why the turntable kept skipping, and nobody complained. One woman stayed under the lemon tree for four hours and read an entire novel. A neighbour brought their dog, and the dog has basically worked here ever since.

That evening, our friends came by with a few bottles of wine. It got cold, so everyone wandered inside. Theo put a record on in the lounge room. Then another. By 11 people were dancing downstairs, someone had started a very serious conversation about jazz on the upstairs couches, and there were six people sitting on the stairs who refused to pick a floor. By midnight we realised we'd accidentally started what are now our [Friday and Saturday nights in the House](/blog/dj-nights-in-fitzroy-what-to-expect/).

## So, what does coffee have to do with the soul?

We're not scientists, and we're not going to tell you coffee will change your life. But after a few years of watching people walk through our door, here's what we've noticed.

- **It gives you a pause.** Waiting for a coffee is one of the few times in a day where nobody expects anything of you. Four minutes to just stand there, smell something good and breathe.
- **It's an excuse to be around people.** Cafes are that rare third place that isn't home and isn't work. You can be alone, together. You can also end up chatting to a stranger about records and leave with a new friend.
- **Someone made it for you.** There's something quietly lovely about a person taking care over a small thing, just for you. Every coffee we make is a tiny act of looking after someone.
- **It's a ritual you can count on.** Life gets loud. A morning coffee at the same garden table is a little bit of steady in the chaos.

> A good coffee won't fix your life. But it might fix your morning, and honestly that's a solid start. *Mia, probably while fixing Doris*

That's the whole idea behind this place. Good coffee, sure. But mostly a leafy, slightly wonky garden where you can slow down, feel at home and leave a little lighter than you came in.

## Come be part of the next chapter

It's still the four of us here most days. Mia's behind the machine, Theo's digging through crates, Ana's out in the garden misting plants that are definitely bigger than last week, and Kofi is fixing something that was perfectly fine yesterday.

If you're in Fitzroy, come and [say hi](/#visit). Grab a flat white, find a spot under the lemon tree, swap a record. And if you want to see where it all really started, come back on a Friday or Saturday night when the House opens up.

We'll save you a spot on the stairs.
