Coffee is a funny thing. On paper it's roasted seeds and hot water. In real life it's the first warm thing you hold in the morning, the excuse to step away from your desk, the reason you finally talk to the person who's been sitting at the next table every Tuesday for a month.

We've spent a lot of time thinking about why a small cup of something brown matters so much to so many people. Turns out the answer is also the story of how this cafe happened. So pull up a chair. This one's a bit long, and it starts with a broken heater.

## It started in a share house on a Tuesday

A few winters ago, four of us were living in a draughty terrace a few streets back from Brunswick Street. The heater had died. The landlord was "looking into it". So we did what anyone would do: we huddled around the kitchen bench and made coffee.

**Mia** had spent ten years making coffee in other people's cafes and owned an espresso machine older than all of us. **Theo** ran a record stall at a Sunday market and had more crates of vinyl than furniture. **Ana** had quietly propagated so many plant cuttings in jam jars that you couldn't see out the windows. And **Kofi** was a carpenter who couldn't walk past a skip bin without rescuing a chair.

That night the machine sputtered, hissed and then refused to work at all. Mia fixed it with a butter knife and a level of stubbornness we've since learned not to argue with. Theo put on a record. Ana made toasties. Kofi fixed the wobbly kitchen stool while we watched.

Somewhere around the third flat white, Ana said, "We should just do this for real."

Everyone laughed. Then nobody said anything for a while.

## The shopfront with the faded sign

A week later, walking home from the pub, we passed an old shoe repair shop that had been empty for so long the *For Lease* sign had faded to a pale pink. Exposed brick. Big front window. A back courtyard full of weeds and possibility.

Kofi called the number the next morning before anyone could talk him out of it.

The owner, a retired seamstress who'd lived in Fitzroy her whole life, walked us through the place, looked at the four of us standing there in our op shop jumpers and said, "You look like trouble. Good. This street needs some." We shook on it over a cup of very bad instant coffee in her kitchen.

We pooled our savings, sold things we probably shouldn't have, and signed the lease with shaking hands.

## Building it by hand (and slightly crooked)

We didn't have money for a fit out, so we made one.

Kofi built the counter out of old floorboards saved from a terrace being knocked down around the corner. Every chair came from hard rubbish, a garage sale or a nan clearing out her shed. Ana's plants moved in first and immediately started climbing the walls. Theo's records lined the back shelf, which is still noticeably wonky. We've decided it's a feature.

There were nights painting until 2am, a burst pipe we don't talk about, and one very tense afternoon trying to get a second hand espresso machine through a front door that was clearly not designed for it. Mia named the machine Doris. Doris is still going.

## Opening day: 31 coffees and one record

We opened on a Saturday with no sign, no printed menu and a chalkboard that just said *coffee, toast, records*.

We sold 31 coffees. Theo played the same album nine times in a row because none of us could work out why the turntable kept skipping, and nobody complained. One woman stayed for four hours and read an entire novel. A neighbour brought their dog, and the dog has basically worked here ever since.

That evening, our friends came by with a few bottles of wine. Theo dragged the decks to the front window and started playing. People stayed. People danced, a bit awkwardly, between the tables. By midnight we realised we'd accidentally started what are now our [Friday and Saturday DJ nights](/blog/dj-nights-in-fitzroy-what-to-expect/).

## So, what does coffee have to do with the soul?

We're not scientists, and we're not going to tell you coffee will change your life. But after a few years of watching people walk through our door, here's what we've noticed.

- **It gives you a pause.** Waiting for a coffee is one of the few times in a day where nobody expects anything of you. Four minutes to just stand there, smell something good and breathe.
- **It's an excuse to be around people.** Cafes are that rare third place that isn't home and isn't work. You can be alone, together. You can also end up chatting to a stranger about records and leave with a new friend.
- **Someone made it for you.** There's something quietly lovely about a person taking care over a small thing, just for you. Every coffee we make is a tiny act of looking after someone.
- **It's a ritual you can count on.** Life gets loud. A morning coffee at the same window seat is a little bit of steady in the chaos.

> A good coffee won't fix your life. But it might fix your morning, and honestly that's a solid start. *Mia, probably while fixing Doris*

That's the whole idea behind this place. Good coffee, sure. But mostly a warm, slightly wonky room where you can slow down, feel at home and leave a little lighter than you came in.

## Come be part of the next chapter

It's still the four of us here most days. Mia's behind the machine, Theo's digging through crates, Ana's misting plants that are definitely bigger than last week, and Kofi is fixing something that was perfectly fine yesterday.

If you're in Fitzroy, come and [say hi](/#visit). Grab a flat white, sit by the window, swap a record. And if you want to see where it all really started, come down on a Friday or Saturday night when the decks come out.

We'll save you a mismatched chair.
